From a SQL sentence to a plan

SQL says what you want, not how to get it. The DBMS parses the sentence, throws out nonsense, and rewrites the algebra so the cheap work happens first.

Part 8 · Plan

SQL names the answer, not the method

Two jobs

SQL is declarative. A query says which rows you want. It does not say whether to scan, use an index, or which join to do first.

  • Query decomposition turns valid SQL into relational algebra and rejects the rest.
  • Query optimisation chooses among equivalent algebra trees.

Four checks before any optimisation

  • Parsing splits the text into tokens and builds a syntax tree.
  • Normalisation rewrites the WHERE into a standard shape.
  • Semantic analysis rejects disconnected or contradictory queries.
  • Simplification checks permission and shrinks the condition with Boolean laws.

Remember: A DBMS first turns SQL into relational algebra, then chooses a cheap equivalent tree.

More detail

Dynamic optimisation replans every time the query runs, using fresh statistics. Static optimisation plans once and reuses the plan. Static is cheaper to run repeatedly; dynamic notices when the data has changed shape.

Source: Lecture 4, learning objectives and “Query processing in RDBMSs”.

Part 8 · Plan

Parsing, then a normal form for the condition

Tokens, then a tree

Lexical analysis checks every word is a known token: a keyword, a name, a number, an operator. A grammar then accepts only legal combinations. The result is an abstract syntax tree, which becomes a relational algebra tree: leaves are tables, inner nodes are operations, the root is the last operation.

A separate check confirms the names exist and the operations fit the types. You cannot average a street name.

Conjunctive normal form

A condition is easier to check when it is a chain of AND of OR-groups. That is conjunctive normal form.

position = 'Manager' AND city = 'London'

is already one AND of two simple clauses.

The lecture also uses a normalised attribute graph to catch contradictions, such as asking for a rent that is both above 500 and below 400.

Remember: Parse into a tree, then rewrite the WHERE into ANDs of ORs so contradictions are easy to see.

More detail

A relation connection graph links tables that the WHERE actually joins, and links the SELECT list back to those tables. If the result columns are not connected to the tables, the query is rejected as badly formed, even when the syntax is legal.

Source: Lecture 4, parsing, normalisation, and semantic analysis.

Part 8 · Plan

The same question, three prices

Managers at a London branch

SELECT *
FROM Staff s, Branch b
WHERE s.branchNo = b.branchNo
  AND s.position = 'Manager'
  AND b.city = 'London';

The lecture assumes 1,000 staff, 50 branches, 50 managers and 5 London branches, no indexes, and one tuple read at a time.

Disk accesses

StrategyWhat it doesAccesses
1Build the full product, then filter101,050
2Join on branch number, then filter3,050
3Select managers and London branches first, then join those small sets1,160

Strategy 3 does about a ninetieth of the work of strategy 1. Same answer.

Remember: Select first, then join the small results. The lecture’s three strategies cost 101,050, 3,050 and 1,160 disk accesses.

More detail

The arithmetic assumes every tuple is its own disk access and that nothing is indexed. An index changes the absolute numbers. It does not change the lesson: a product of two big tables is the expensive object to avoid.

Source: Lecture 4, “Example of importance of query optimization” and the cost comparison.

Part 8 · Plan

Rules that keep the answer and change the cost

The rewrites you are expected to use

  • A selection of an AND splits: σp AND q(R) = σp(σq(R)), and the two selections commute.
  • A product plus a selection on the join columns is a join.
  • A selection moves across a product or a join when its columns all come from one side.
  • A cascade of projections collapses to the outermost one. A projection can also be pushed down.
  • Union, intersection and product are commutative and associative. Difference is not.

How the lecture applies them

Start from the tree the parser built: a product, then one big selection, then a projection.

  • Split the selection and push each piece onto the table it belongs to.
  • Turn the remaining product-plus-equality into an equijoin.
  • Join the most selective pair first.
  • Push projections down so later joins carry fewer columns.

Remember: Push selections and projections inward, and turn a filtered product into a join. Do the most selective join first.

More detail

Intersection can be written with difference: R ∩ S = R − (R − S). A natural join is a product, a selection on the shared names, and a projection that drops the duplicate columns. Those are equivalences, not performance tips by themselves.

Source: Lecture 4, “Relational algebra transformation rules” and the worked tree.

71

Part 8 · Plan

The heuristic, in one line

Do the shrinking work early

Selections and projections make tables smaller. The lecture’s heuristic is to perform them as early as possible, using the rules to move them inward, and to combine a selection with a product into a join whenever you can.

Cascades of the same unary operation collapse. A chain of projections is just the last projection.

What this part does not yet decide

The heuristic picks a promising tree. It does not yet estimate disk reads from statistics, and it does not yet choose between a nested loop and a hash join. That is Part 9.

Remember: Shrink the tables before you combine them. The algebra rules are how you move the work without changing the answer.

More detail

The summary slide’s two sentences are the exam version: parsing builds a relational algebra tree, and different trees for the same query can differ enormously in cost, so the DBMS rewrites toward selections and projections early.

Source: Lecture 4, “Heuristics for speeding up queries” and the summary.
